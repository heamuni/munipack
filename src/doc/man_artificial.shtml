Artificial Frames

This tool is designed to create the artificial, nevertheless photometric realistic, frames. They are intended for modelling of various observation effects as well as for testing purposes.

Synopsis

munipack artificial [.. parameters ..]

Description

Artificial frames are created by artificial photons having origin in physical principles and mathematical formulas rather than in nature. This approach can be very useful for understanding of true nature of many effects which can be else gathered by a plenty of observational experiences.

There is list of currently implemented features:

  • Quantum efficiency of detector, telescope parameters.
  • Atmospheric extinction (monochromatic)
  • Astronomical Seeing
  • Point spread functions (PSF): Gaussian, Moffat and seeing.
  • Sky background, dark current, photon (Poisson) and read-out noise
  • Light curves: plain wave, Algol-like, δ Cep and user defined
  • Field stars: by a catalogue or generated randomly.

The frames are generated:

  • on base of catalogue stars which simulates nearly real field or
  • on base of randomly generated positions and magnitudes of stars.

Modelling of Telescope Properties

The properties of star images can be easy simulated for various quantum efficiency η of a detection apparatus (including detector itself), an exposure duration and a telescope diameter.

Both angular resolution and collecting area of a telescoped are proportional to diameter. The parameter --airy can set it directly, but better way is set diameter (via --diameter in meters) which sets both diffraction limit and input area.

Modelling of Extinction

An observed light intensity I(X) passing the clear (no clouds) Earth's atmosphere is attenuated proportionally of air-mass X according to the formula

I(X) = I0 e-k(X-1)

where I0 is an extra-atmospheric intensity. The extinction coefficient k depends on a spectral band.

The sky brightness (see below) increases with air-mass: the light emitting layer (air-glow, scattered light) is seen through a longer path, proportional to X in the plane-parallel approximation, while the light is attenuated by the extinction as for stars (bZ is its zenit value)

b(X) = bZ X e-k(X-1).

Modelling of seeing

Turbulent motion in atmosphere creates large cells which slightly reflect light rays. The nature of turbulent motion is totally chaotic. There is a formula describing effect of chaotic moving of picture of a star due atmosphere during long exposures. It modelled star profile as function of air mass.

By Mr.Fries theory (very nice description has Electronic Imaging in Astronomy by Ian S. McLean), the seeing radius is modelled as

r'0 = r0 X0.6.

r'0 is an actual radius of stars at air-mass X, while r0 is the one at zenith.

Modelling of Star Profiles

These profiles known as point spread functions (PSF) are available:

  • Seeing spread function which convoluted diffraction pattern of telescope aperture and Gaussian spread by seeing. It is the best method for simulation of any star natural profile.
  • Moffat profile which is commonly used for rough description of observed profiles.
  • Gaussian profiles exp(-r2/2σ2) which is the basic estimator. It is ideal for theoretical work; any practical use is limited on extra-atmospheric applications, such as spacecraft or Moon base observatories, without image perturbations by the seeing.

The plain diffraction pattern, representing response of an input aperture of a telescope without atmosphere seeing, can be generated as seeing spread with zero seeing parameter. It is difficult to expose it, even in laboratory, due to limited dynamical range of detectors.

The distribution of seeing is supposed as Gaussian and convoluted with actual diffraction profile. There are two methods convolution implemented:

  • Direct convolution -- diffraction profile is randomly shifted in both directions and the result is accumulated. This slow method gives slightly asymmetrical frames and is intended for simulating of short exposures.
  • Fourier convolution -- this method generates both diffraction profile and Gaussian with the Fried's width. The profiles are convoluted with help of convolution theorem and FFT. This method is fast and results are perfectly smooth.

All stars, on the image, are modelled with the same profile.

Elliptic star profiles

Non-circular star profiles are modelled by ellipse. Any ellipse is characterised by semi-major axis a (having radius meaning r0) and semi-minor axis b. b can not be specified directly: eccentricity 0 ≤ e < 1 is used instead; the parameter squeezes circle (e=0, a = b) to a line (e → 1).

Ellipsis is oriented to have its major semi-axis parallel to horizontal Cartesian axis by default. It can be optionally rotated by angle -90° < i ≤ 90°.

Non-circularity can be used to model imperfections in telescope tracking.

Modelling of Background and Detector Noise

The frame is generated in electrons (photo-electrons and thermal electrons). The mean (noiseless) number of electrons in the pixel x, y is

λ(x,y) = F(x,y) [ B(x,y) + S(x,y) ] + D t,

where S(x,y) are stars (and galaxies), F(x,y) is the flat-field model (--flat-model-file, unity by default), D is the dark current in electrons per second and pixel (--dark) and t is the exposure time. The dark current is uniform: it is not affected by the flat-field, the sky gradient, air-mass, area or quantum efficiency.

The sky background is

B(x,y) = B0 10-0.4 [gx (x - x0) + gy (y - y0)],

where B0 is the level at the centre of the frame x0, y0 and gx, gy are the gradients of the sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond and pixel (--sky-grad-x, --sky-grad-y); positive values mean a fainter sky (less counts) in the direction. The gradient can model a light pollution (by Moon). The common values for gradients are of order 0.0001 … 0.001 mag/arcsec²/pix. The level is

B0 = √(b² + g²) t A η s²,

where b is the photon rate of the sky (per second, square meter and square arcsecond) derived from --sky-mag for the filter, g is the additional (deprecated) background rate given by --gnoise in the same units, A is the area, η the quantum efficiency and s is the pixel size in arcseconds.

The frame with the noise is

I(x,y) = [ Po(λ(x,y)) + N(0, σR) ] / G,

Po(λ) is a random number with the Poisson distribution (the photon or shot noise of all the electrons; the variance equals to the mean λ), N(0, σR) is the read-out noise with the Normal distribution and the standard deviation σR in electrons (--readnoise), independent on the exposure time. Cosmic rays are added after the Poisson step. G is the gain in electrons per ADU (--gain), the result is clipped by the saturation (--saturate), and also at zero for integer (-B 8, 16, 32) frames. Both the Poisson and the read-out noise are switched-off by --disable-noise (the frame is λ(x,y)/G then). The variance of a background pixel is (λ + σR2) / G2 in ADU².

Star fields

Field stars are generated by a random number generator, if no -c option (a catalogue) is presented. Catalogue star coordinates and magnitudes are used otherwise.

Rectangular (without projection) coordinates of the stars has uniform distribution in ranges given by a frame size. The standard Fortran random number generator is used. It is initialised randomly, unless --seed is given.

Magnitude distribution, meaning a probability to found number of stars in given magnitude interval per square degree, is modelled as an exponential function with a cut-off to prevent Olbersʼ paradox. I derived a rough approximation of the observed (surface) distribution of stars by brightens from DR1 by Gaia mission:

Σ(m) = 10-4 · e1.1m [□°],   m < m0,

(the symbol □° designates square degree). The cut-off m0 is given by --maglim parameter. Large values of m0 and large field of view can effectively exhaust the computer memory.

The distribution approximation must be considered as an effective description. Stars are distributed over our sky very non homogeneously. Standard galaxy textbooks (like Binney's & Merrifield's Galactic Astronomy) gives more extensive description with references.

Models of light curves

The artificial tool has three models of light curves included. The model of the pulsating star δ Cep (data computed by Mérand et al.) and the eclipsing binary star Algol (observed by Kim, Ho-Il). Both the curves has been approximated by Fourier series. The last model is a simple cosine wave.

There is also possibility to define own Fourier series approximation of light curves which is suitable for wave-like patterns. Do create a FITS table with format similar to fourier.lst. The table contains real Fourier coefficients an, bn. If the coefficients are estimated from measured data with noise, any appearance of high-order terms indicates inappropriateness of Fourier approximation. I can't recommend use on curves with sharp edges as Algol-like objects has. Note. The Fourier approximation gives very good approximation of Cepheids but is not very suitable for Algoids. The spline approximation can be recommended in that case.

Universal flux-time pattern is implemented as a time series table. It can represent a periodic object like Algol star or exoplanet transition, but it can also model an non periodic behaviour like a supernova explosion or an optical afterglow. The usage is described in Light Curves section of Overview.

Galaxy models

This artificial tool implements models of elliptical galaxies. A radial profile have Sérsic profile (generalised de Vaucouleurs profile). A shape (nearly circular or nearly lenticular) is given by the Hubble classification; an orientation and shape of ellipticity is the same as for PSF of stars. All parameters are specified in the particular galaxy catalogue file. See Artificial sky for examples.

Watermark

Every generated frame is marked by a symbol which indicates its artificial origin to prevent potential confusion. The mark can not be hide.

Parameters

--psf [SEEING|MOFFAT|GAUSS]
Selects PSF function: SEEING (the best, slow, default), MOFFAT (commonly used), GAUSS (basic model, fast). Other names are rejected.
--psf-file filename
PSF by the external FITS file (specification).
--spread [AUTO|FFT|RANDOM]
The method used to spread diffraction pattern (useful with --psf SEEING only): FFT (fast, smooth), RANDOM (slow, natural look) or AUTO (the RANDOM is selected for exposures shorter than 3 seconds)
--hwhm width
The width parameter of PSF in pixels, common for all PSF. Despite its name, it is not the half-width at half of maximum: it is the standard deviation σ of the Gaussian for GAUSS and of the seeing spread for SEEING (HWHM = 1.1774 σ), and the parameter α of (1 + r²/α²)-β for MOFFAT (HWHM = α √(21/β - 1)).
--airy airy
Radius of Airy spot in pixels. It is useful for seeing PSF. This parameter can be also set by --diameter.
--beta β
Value of β exponent of Moffat profile (β > 0, β ≠ 1).
--eccentricity e
Eccentricity of PSF by a contour ellipse: 0 ≤ e < 1. The default value e=0 specifies a circular PSF.
--inclination i
Inclination of major semi-axis of the ellipse in degrees. Positive values of the angle are in mathematics sense (counter-clockwise).
--maglim m0
Sets the most faint stars on frame for random generated field stars. This option is active only when star catalogue (by -c,--cat) is not used.
--lc-model [Cepheid|Algol|wave]
The light curve model: `Cepheid' means light curve of δ Cep, `Algol' means β Per, `wave' means cosine function. This parameter is mutually exclusive with --lc-table, --lc-fourier.
--lc-table file
The light curve model is determined by the smoothing spline interpolation in De Boor's approach of the table values. The table format is described below. This parameter is mutually exclusive with --lc-model, --lc-fourier.
--lc-fourier file
The light curve model is given by the coefficients of Fourier series listed in a FITS table (see below). This parameter is mutually exclusive with --lc-model, --lc-table.
--lc-mag m0, --lc-amp A
The magnitude zero point and the amplitude for a light curve. Magnitudes are derived from computed fluxes F as m = m0 + 2.5 log AF.
--lc-jd0 jd0, --lc-per period
Time elements of the light curve: jd0 is a reference time in Julian date (exact meaning depends on the kind of the curve), period in days. Don't use modified JD. The light curve (as well as the air-mass) is evaluated at the half of exposure (the JD keyword).
--lc-ra α, --lc-dec δ
A Right Ascension and Declination of the variable source. If the coordinates are undefined, the centre of projection (as --rcen, --dcen) is used. Both the coordinates must be given together.
--sky-mag skymag
Set sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond. Default value is 21 meaning good observing conditions. Set --sky-mag 99 to effectivelly switch it off.
--sky-grad-x xmag, --sky-grad-y ymag
Gradient of the sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond and pixel in given direction, relative to the frame centre; positive values make the sky fainter in the direction. The order of common values is in the interval 0.0001 … 0.001. It simulates a light pollution (by Moon). The dark current is not affected.
--dark rate
Dark current in electrons per second and pixel (default: 0). It adds rate × exptime electrons to the mean of every pixel, uniformly (unaffected by the sky gradient, air-mass, area, quantum efficiency or the flat-field). Its noise is Poisson, together with the sky. The value is recorded in the DARKCUR keyword.
--readnoise σ
Read-out noise in electrons (rms, default: 0). A Gaussian noise N(0,σ) is added independently to every pixel after the Poisson noise and before the division by gain. It does not change the mean and does not depend on the exposure time. The value is recorded in the RDNOISE keyword.
--gnoise rate
Deprecated, use --dark and --readnoise. Despite its name, it is not a Gaussian noise: it is an additional background photon rate in photons per second, square meter and square arcsecond, which is merged in quadrature with the sky rate (given by --sky-mag): the background level is √(sky² + gnoise²) × exptime × area × qeff × pixel area [arcsec²]. It is handled as the sky (including the gradient and air-mass). The noise is Poisson on the total.
--seed n
An integer seed of the random number generator. With the seed, the generator is re-initialised from the pair (seed, frame index) at the start of every frame (and by the seed for the random star field), so the frames are reproducible and independent on the number of parallel processes (--mproc). Without the seed, the generator is initialised randomly. The value is recorded in the ARTSEED keyword.
--area area
The detection area of a simulated telescope in square meters. It is 0.985 m² by default, the area of 1.12 m diameter (see --diameter option). If --diameter is not given, the area also determines the radius of the aperture used for the Airy radius (unless --airy is given).
--diameter diameter
Sets diameter (twice of radius) of a telescope in meters. If set, the area (unless --area is given) and the Airy radius (unless --airy is given) are derived from it.
--exptime time
The exposure time of generated frames in seconds.
--qeff η
The quantum efficiency of a whole apparatus (0 ≤ η ≤ 1).
--disable-noise
Switch-off all the sources of noise: the photon (Poisson) noise of stars, sky and dark current and the read-out noise; the frames contain the mean values. Cosmic rays are not affected. This switch is intended for some algorithm testing.
--atmosphere
Switch-on modelling of the atmosphere. The model includes both extinction and seeing.
--extk k
Monochromatic extinction coefficient. Setting on zero effectively suppress of the extinction determination.
--long λ, --lat φ
Geographic coordinates of a station in degrees (+east, +north). Coordinates of Brno observatory are specified as --long +16.6, --lat +49.2. Both the coordinates must be given together.
--date YYYY-MM-DD, --time HH:MM:SS
Date as YYYY-MM-DD and time as HH:MM:SS of (initial) frame.
--count #
Total count of generated frames.
--timestep time
Time delay between simulated exposures in seconds.
-f, --filter filter
Simulated filter.
--gain gain
The value of gain meaning (photo-)electrons per ADU. The frames are in ADU while the COUNT column of the CATALOGUE extension is the mean number of electrons of the star (before the flat-field and gain).
--saturate saturate
The saturation, or the maximal value in the frame, the equivalent of the full-well capacity.
--cosmics-freq freq
Cosmic-rays frequency per one second. The number of events is drawn from the Poisson distribution with the mean freq × exptime for every frame.
--cosmics-cmax cmax
The cosmic-rays maximal intensity in electrons.
--cosmics-len length
The maximal lengths of cosmics rays.
-c, --cat file
Input catalogue with star positions and magnitudes.
--galcat file
Input catalogue with galaxy models (format)
--fov fov
Field of view in degrees. If specified, the scale (--scale) is superseded.
-p, --projection code
The code of celestial projections. TAN is for the gnomonical projection (the default one). The codes should be recognised by WCSLIB.
--rcen α, --dcen δ
Centre of the field of view in Right Ascension and Declination in degrees. Both the coordinates must be given together.
--scale s
Scale of the frame in pixels per degree (3600 by default, meaning one arcsecond per pixel).
--angle φ
Angle of rotation of the frame around the centre in degrees. Clockwise direction is positive.
--width width, --height height
Dimensions of output frame in pixels. Both must be given together.
--fits-keys-file file
The file contains additional FITS keys. The a plain text file is in the same form as a template.
--flat-model-file fitsfile
A FITS file as a model a flat-field, i.e. the per pixel sensitivity due both camera and telescope. The frame dimensions must correspond to the artificial image.

Examples

  $ munipack artificial
  $ xmunipack artificial.fits

Overview comes with more useful examples.

Light curve table

The table contains the light curve representing dependence of relative fluxes (i.e. not in magnitudes) on the phase. The tree column format of the Time Series Table is assumed. Columns are: the phase, the flux, and the flux statistical error. The first column with is the phase. The second column is the light curve represented in relative fluxes. The third column is a uncertainty of the flux (standard deviations) as positive numbers. Records in the table must be sorted in time-increasing order. The interpolated value is combined with --lc-mag, --lc-amp, --lc-jd0, --lc-per. Overview describes its application.

Table of Fourier coefficients

The table contains the Fourier approximation of a light curve in the fluxes (i.e. not in magnitudes) for a single period. There are two columns representing the real (first) and the imaginary (second) parts of coefficients in the Fourier series. The n row of the table corresponds to an-1,bn-1 coefficients. The determined value is combined with --lc-mag, --lc-amp, --lc-jd0, --lc-per. The text file fourier.lst demonstrates an instance of data; a FITS file, required by --lc-fourier, is created as 

  $ munipack fits --restore fourier.lst
